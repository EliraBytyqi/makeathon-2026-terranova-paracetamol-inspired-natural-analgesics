Paracetamol-inspired natural analgesics

Paracetamol relieves pain and damages livers through two chemically separable routes. We screened 145,000 plant natural products for the first while filtering out the second.

The hypothesis

Two pathways from one molecule

Paracetamol's analgesia is increasingly attributed not to COX inhibition but to its metabolite AM404. Its hepatotoxicity runs through an entirely different route. That separation is the whole opportunity.

Analgesia — the route we want

paracetamol → p-aminophenol → AM404

Deacetylation, then conjugation with arachidonic acid by FAAH. AM404 acts centrally and in the spinal dorsal horn through TRPV1 and the cannabinoid system.

TRPV1 CB1 FAAH

Hepatotoxicity — the route we filter

paracetamol → CYP2E1 / 3A4 → NAPQI

Oxidation of the para-aminophenol core to a hard electrophile. At dose, glutathione conjugates it; in overdose that pool depletes, NAPQI arylates protein thiols, and you get centrilobular necrosis.

quinone-imine precursor thiol arylation
The design question. Both arms begin at the same para-aminophenol core, but only the second requires it. So we look for plant compounds that engage TRPV1 / CB1 / FAAH without carrying the structural features that enable bioactivation — and we check that structurally, not just statistically.

The ethnopharmacology seed list

51 hand-curated species with a traditional pain indication and a mechanistic hypothesis. Compounds from these plants are tiered pain_seed; everything else from any land plant is plant_background, so "top ranked" means something.

Data & provenance

Four datasets, three public sources

Every filtering decision happens in one place and is counted. Hidden curation is how cheminformatics papers become irreproducible.

Curation attrition

Raw ChEMBL records to modelling-ready rows, counted at every step. Hover any bar for what was dropped there.

The biggest single cut is activity type: only IC50, EC50, Ki and Kd survive. Percent-inhibition at a single concentration is not convertible to a potency, so it cannot be labelled consistently.

What survived, per target

Active ≤ 1 µM, inactive ≥ 10 µM. The 1–10 µM band is discarded, not forced into a class — those compounds are genuinely ambiguous and training on them teaches noise.

The active rate is publication bias, not biology

ChEMBL is assembled from medicinal-chemistry papers, and people publish compounds that worked. A classifier trained on 84% actives learns that nearly everything is active, and its ranking over 145k natural products is then driven by noise. Class weighting does not fix this: the model has simply never been shown what a non-ligand looks like.

The fix: 60,000 drug-like molecules sampled across ChEMBL's whole ID range as presumed inactives, property-matched to each target's actives on MW and cLogP (DUD-E style), excluding anything sharing a scaffold with a known active. Three decoys per active brings ~75% down to ~20%. They stay labelled presumed_inactive so results can be re-derived without the assumption.

Models

Nine models, evaluated twice each

Every model is scored under a random split and a scaffold split. The gap between them is the honest measure of what happens when you screen chemistry the model has never seen.

Why the split matters more than the score

ChEMBL is built from congeneric series — one paper, one scaffold, many analogues. Under a random split those analogues straddle train and test, the model memorises the series, and you report an ROC-AUC near 0.95 that collapses on anything new. Natural products share almost no scaffolds with ChEMBL, so the random number is the wrong measurement for this project. Both are reported below; the scaffold number is the one that counts.

All model metrics

PR-AUC-led because the data is imbalanced and only the top of the ranked list matters. EF 1% is the screening use case: hand a chemist the top 1%, how many more actives than random?

Architecture

Representation

ECFP4 (Morgan, radius 2, 2048 bits) plus physicochemical descriptors. Every structure in the project — ChEMBL, decoys, LOTUS, DILI — passes through the same standardize(), so they are comparable.

Algorithms

Random Forest (500 trees) and LightGBM (600 estimators) are both trained. The one with the better scaffold-split PR-AUC wins and is refit on all data for deployment.

Applicability domain

Each model ships its training-set fingerprint matrix, so every prediction carries a max-Tanimoto similarity to what the model has actually seen. Below 0.25 a prediction is extrapolation and is flagged, not silently trusted. This is why the model files are 50–200 MB.

FAAH is labelled twice

The natural-product FAAH inhibitors motivating this project sit at 1–15 µM. A 1 µM cut labels nearly all of them inactive, training the model to reject exactly the chemistry we are hunting. FAAH therefore also gets a 10 µM variant, and both are reported.

The FAAH rescue experiment

Six known plant FAAH inhibitors, none of them in ChEMBL's FAAH set. Each is scored by a model that never saw it: once by a model trained on ChEMBL alone, once by a model that additionally saw only the other five.

The DILI model, and what it gets wrong

Random Forest over the TDC DILI benchmark — 475 marketed drugs (462 after standardisation), aggregated by the FDA's National Center for Toxicological Research. Evaluated over five repeated scaffold splits, because one split on 462 compounds is noisy.

Paracetamol is not in the benchmark at all

Neither is isoniazid — two of the most famous hepatotoxins in medicine. So the model scores paracetamol 0.37, i.e. low risk, and it is wrong for a structural reason: the benchmark encodes idiosyncratic, immune-mediated DILI rather than intrinsic dose-dependent toxicity. A statistical model that misses this project's own index compound has not earned a large say in the ranking — which is exactly why the structural alert layer carries the weight instead.
External validation

The only genuinely held-out test

26 compound–target claims from the primary literature, 16 of them absent from ChEMBL training entirely. Scored by models that have never seen them. This is the honest test, and it is the most important result in the project.

Read this before you read anything else

Not one held-out literature active scores above 0.5. The median predicted probability is effectively zero, and the median library percentile is 63 — barely above the 50 you would get by chance. The scaffold-split PR-AUC of 0.88–0.98 did not transfer to real plant chemistry.

This is the number most projects never compute, and reporting it is the point. The scaffold split controls for memorising chemical series; it does not control for the training set never having contained this kind of molecule at all. Only an external set exposes that, and it did.

Where the models do and do not generalise

Ranked by percentile against the full 145k library background — the actual screening question. Hover any bar for the reported potency.

The pattern is not random. The four compounds that do surface — 6-gingerol, curcumin, eugenol, piperine — are all TRPV1 vanilloids, structurally close to capsaicin, which is in training. The FAAH flavonoids all sit mid-pack. The models extrapolate a little way from what they know, and no further.

Every claim, scored

The "seen in training" group is a wiring check, not evidence: median probability 0.9996 confirms the pipeline scores known ligands correctly end to end.

The diagnosis, and why the project is not sunk by this

The FAAH rescue experiment answers exactly the question this result raises. Adding five curated literature actives moves the median prediction on a held-out sixth from 3.5e-5 to 0.33 — four orders of magnitude. So the failure is coverage, not capability. ECFP4 plus a gradient-boosted tree can represent this chemistry; ChEMBL simply does not contain it.

That turns a negative result into a concrete, costed next step: the fix is a few hundred curated literature actives, not a new architecture. Stating that is worth more than a PR-AUC nobody can reproduce.

Safety

Two layers, because one is not enough

A structural alert set that encodes bioactivation chemistry, and a statistical DILI model. They disagree about paracetamol, and that disagreement is the point.

How the composite score is built

Deliberately transparent, because an opaque ranking formula is unfalsifiable. The weights are printed in the output and shown here.

composite = target_score × exp(−0.35 × alert_severity_sum) × ad_confidence × cns_bonus × (1 − 0.30 × p_dili)
The DILI term is deliberately small relative to the structural alert term. The alerts flag paracetamol at maximum severity on the exact NAPQI mechanism; the model calls it low risk. We weighted the layer that is right about the compound the whole project is named after.

Reactive-metabolite alert set

25 SMARTS patterns, read live from src/safety.py. Severity 3 is a direct NAPQI-type bioactivation precedent; 2 is an established reactive-metabolite route; 1 is context-dependent.

Two caveats that belong in the writeup

Structural alerts are a hazard flag, not a risk assessment. Dose, exposure, clearance route and detoxification capacity all matter and none are encoded here. A flagged compound is not "toxic"; it is "worth a reactivity check".

The alert set flags compounds this project likes. Curcumin and 6-gingerol are Michael acceptors. Many flavonoids are catechols. That is a genuine finding about the chemical space of plant analgesics — plants make electrophiles on purpose, often as defence chemistry — not a bug to be tuned away. We report the flags and keep the compounds, tagged.

ML decoder

This project, in terms you already know

Written for an ML engineer with no biology background. Every piece of chemistry here maps onto something standard — grouped splits, negative sampling, OOD detection, lift@k. The domain words are the only new part.

The task in one paragraph

We have three binary classifiers, one per protein target. Input is a molecule; output is P(this molecule acts on this protein). Training labels come from a public database of lab measurements, where a continuous potency value is thresholded into active/inactive. We then run inference over 145,164 unlabelled molecules found in plants and rank them. A fourth classifier predicts liver toxicity, and a rule-based filter removes molecules containing substructures known to cause it. The final ranking is a hand-weighted reranker over all of it.

It is a retrieval problem

Not accuracy. We hand a chemist the top 50 of 145k and they test those. Only the head of the ranking is ever seen, which is why every metric is PR-AUC and lift@k rather than accuracy or ROC.

The test set is deliberately OOD

Training data is synthetic drug chemistry; inference data is plant chemistry. They barely overlap. So the evaluation uses a grouped split on molecular core structure, and every prediction carries a novelty score.

Labels are badly biased

The source database is built from published papers, and people publish what worked. The raw positive rate is 84%. That is publication bias, not base rate, and it is the single biggest data problem in the project.

What each model is for

Nine pickles in models/. Eight are target classifiers, one is the toxicity model. They differ only in which rows of the same table they were trained on.

Why two scopes. human trains only on human-protein measurements; all adds rat and mouse. More data versus a cleaner label — the classic trade. Both are trained and reported rather than picking one on a hunch.

Why FAAH has two label sets. The decision threshold on the continuous potency value is normally 1 µM. The plant compounds we are hunting sit at 1–15 µM, so a 1 µM threshold labels almost all of them negative — training the model to reject precisely the chemistry we want. The label_10um variant moves the threshold. This is threshold selection driven by the deployment distribution, not by the training distribution.
