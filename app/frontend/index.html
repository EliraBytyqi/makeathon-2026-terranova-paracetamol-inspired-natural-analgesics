Paracetamol-inspired natural analgesics

Paracetamol relieves pain and damages livers through two chemically separable routes. We screened 145,000 plant natural products for the first while filtering out the second.

The hypothesis

Two pathways from one molecule

Paracetamol's analgesia is increasingly attributed not to COX inhibition but to its metabolite AM404. Its hepatotoxicity runs through an entirely different route. That separation is the whole opportunity.

Analgesia — the route we want

paracetamol → p-aminophenol → AM404

Deacetylation, then conjugation with arachidonic acid by FAAH. AM404 acts centrally and in the spinal dorsal horn through TRPV1 and the cannabinoid system.

TRPV1 CB1 FAAH

Hepatotoxicity — the route we filter

paracetamol → CYP2E1 / 3A4 → NAPQI

Oxidation of the para-aminophenol core to a hard electrophile. At dose, glutathione conjugates it; in overdose that pool depletes, NAPQI arylates protein thiols, and you get centrilobular necrosis.

quinone-imine precursor thiol arylation
The design question. Both arms begin at the same para-aminophenol core, but only the second requires it. So we look for plant compounds that engage TRPV1 / CB1 / FAAH without carrying the structural features that enable bioactivation — and we check that structurally, not just statistically.

Rodent orthologs are collected because much published TRPV1 and FAAH pharmacology is rodent, but they are tagged by organism and never silently pooled — TRPV1 has well-documented species differences in ligand sensitivity.

The ethnopharmacology seed list

51 hand-curated species with a traditional pain indication and a mechanistic hypothesis. Compounds from these plants are tiered pain_seed; everything else from any land plant is plant_background, so "top ranked" means something.

Data & provenance

Four datasets, three public sources

Every filtering decision happens in one place and is counted. Hidden curation is how cheminformatics papers become irreproducible.

Curation attrition

Raw ChEMBL records to modelling-ready rows, counted at every step. Hover any bar for what was dropped there.

The biggest single cut is activity type: only IC50, EC50, Ki and Kd survive. Percent-inhibition at a single concentration is not convertible to a potency, so it cannot be labelled consistently.

What survived, per target

Active ≤ 1 µM, inactive ≥ 10 µM. The 1–10 µM band is discarded, not forced into a class — those compounds are genuinely ambiguous and training on them teaches noise.

The active rate is publication bias, not biology

The curated ChEMBL measurements are enriched for actives because medicinal- chemistry studies often focus on compounds that worked. Training on these measured records alone would leave too few negatives for a useful ranking, so we supplement them with carefully selected presumed-inactive decoys.

How we handle the imbalance: training uses one property-matched presumed-inactive decoy per active, with decoy examples weighted at 0.4 because they were not experimentally confirmed inactive. Decoys are excluded if they share a Bemis–Murcko scaffold with a known active, and remain labelled presumed_inactive so their contribution is explicit. We chose this setting after comparing decoy ratios against external PubChem measurements; three decoys per active is a historical experiment, not the current setting.

Models

Nine models, evaluated twice each

Every model is scored under a random split and a scaffold split. The gap between them is the honest measure of what happens when you screen chemistry the model has never seen.

Why the split matters more than the score

ChEMBL is built from congeneric series — one paper, one scaffold, many analogues. Under a random split those analogues straddle train and test, the model memorises the series, and you report an ROC-AUC near 0.95 that collapses on anything new. Natural products share almost no scaffolds with ChEMBL, so the random number is the wrong measurement for this project. Both are reported below; the scaffold number is the one that counts.

All model metrics

PR-AUC-led because the data is imbalanced and only the top of the ranked list matters. EF 1% is the screening use case: hand a chemist the top 1%, how many more actives than random?

Architecture

Representation

ECFP4 (Morgan, radius 2, 2048 bits) plus physicochemical descriptors. Every structure in the project — ChEMBL, decoys, LOTUS, DILI — passes through the same standardize(), so they are comparable.

Algorithms

Random Forest (500 trees) and LightGBM (600 estimators) are both trained. The one with the better scaffold-split PR-AUC wins and is refit on all data for deployment.

Applicability domain

Each model ships its training-set fingerprint matrix, so every prediction carries a max-Tanimoto similarity to what the model has actually seen. Below 0.25 a prediction is extrapolation and is flagged, not silently trusted. This is why the model files are 50–200 MB.

FAAH is labelled twice

The natural-product FAAH inhibitors motivating this project sit at 1–15 µM. A 1 µM cut labels nearly all of them inactive, training the model to reject exactly the chemistry we are hunting. FAAH therefore also gets a 10 µM variant, and both are reported.

The FAAH rescue experiment

Six known plant FAAH inhibitors, none of them in ChEMBL's FAAH set. Each is scored by a model that never saw it: once by a model trained on ChEMBL alone, once by a model that additionally saw only the other five.

External validation

The only genuinely held-out test

Compounds whose activity is reported in the primary literature but which are absent from ChEMBL training, scored by models that never saw them. The result is inconclusive — and the reason why turned out to be the most useful thing we learned.

Validated against real experiments

PubChem BioAssay records Inactive outcomes as well as Active ones, so compounds that were screened and did nothing are in the data. That gives a real denominator — and the only properly controlled test in this project. Every compound below was excluded from training.

Where the models do and do not generalise

All 16 held-out claims by percentile against the full 145k library — the actual screening question. Compounds below their model’s own cut-off are marked; the model is right to score those low.

The pattern is not random. The compounds that do surface — 6-gingerol, curcumin, eugenol, piperine — are all TRPV1 vanilloids, structurally close to capsaicin, which is in training. The FAAH flavonoids sit mid-pack. The models extrapolate a short way from what they know, and no further.

We tested the obvious explanation, and ruled it out

FAAH carries a 10 µM label variant because plant inhibitors sit at 1–15 µM. TRPV1 and CB1 never got one — so we built them, with decoys matched, and re-scored.

Every claim, scored

The “seen in training” group is a wiring check, not evidence: median probability 0.9996 confirms the pipeline scores known ligands correctly end to end.

The diagnosis: coverage, not capability

The FAAH rescue answers the question this result raises. Adding five curated literature actives moves the median prediction on a held-out sixth from 3.5e-5 to 0.330 — four orders of magnitude. The features and the model can represent this chemistry; ChEMBL does not contain it.

That turns an inconclusive test into a costed next step: a few hundred curated literature actives, plus a validation set large enough to actually settle the question. Nine compounds never could.

Safety

Two layers, because one is not enough

A structural alert set that encodes bioactivation chemistry, and a statistical DILI model. They disagree about paracetamol, and that disagreement is the point.

How the composite score is built

Deliberately transparent, because an opaque ranking formula is unfalsifiable. The weights are printed in the output and shown here.

composite = target_score × exp(−0.35 × alert_severity_sum) × ad_confidence × cns_bonus × (1 − 0.35 × p_hepatic)
The hepatic term is weighted 0.35, and that number moved twice. It was 0.30 while the old model could not see its own index compound. The new panel is 27× larger and does contain paracetamol, which first suggested 0.45 — but external validation put the panel near 0.65, not the 0.79 its internal scaffold split implied, so it settled at 0.35. The structural alerts keep the hard veto: they encode mechanism rather than correlation, and they are the only layer that can see metabolism-dependent toxicity at all.

Reactive-metabolite alert set

25 SMARTS patterns, read live from src/safety.py. Severity 3 is a direct NAPQI-type bioactivation precedent; 2 is an established reactive-metabolite route; 1 is context-dependent.
